# Juxtapose: story bible

## The one-line version

You are the **Figment**, a wooden figure from a painting that was never finished. You wake up inside the dreams of the old woman who has kept that painting under a sheet for sixty years. Every night you fight your way down to where it is hidden, before she wakes.

## The Figment (you)

- **What you are.** A jointed wooden *lay figure*, the kind of artist's mannequin painters pose instead of a person. You wear a black bowler hat and a red scarf. Where your face should be, a green apple hangs in the air. You have no face because the painter never got as far as painting one.
- **Where you come from.** You are the subject of *L'Homme au Chapeau* ("The Man in the Hat"), a canvas that **Théo Vautrin** began in the winter of 1958 and abandoned when he left town. He posed a wooden lay figure for the body, and you are that figure, remembered.
- **What you want.** You don't know at first. You wake on the sand with a two-barrelled gun that takes the qualities of things and gives them to other things, and a pull downward. You want to be finished.
- **How you move.** Like someone who has been kept still for a very long time and is finally allowed to run.
- **The gun.** The **Juxtaposition Gun** is a brass-and-walnut antique that belonged to nobody. Its upper barrel, a copper gramophone bell, *takes*. Its lower barrel, ringed with glowing glass, *gives*. A bayonet shaped like a palette knife folds out beneath it. The vial on top shows whatever quality you are carrying. Théo used to say a painting is just two things put next to each other until they mean a third.
- **The twist, revealed at the end.** The painting was never of a man. Théo painted his older sister Odile, who, when they were children, used to wear their father's bowler hat and hold an apple in front of her face to make him laugh. You are Odile, as her brother saw her.

## The dreamer

- **Who she is.** **Odile Vautrin**, 78. She was a clock restorer and lives alone above her shuttered shop on the Rue des Horloges. She is ill and sleeps most of the day now. Early vignettes only call her "the dreamer"; her name surfaces with the memories.
- **Théo.** Her younger brother was a would-be surrealist painter, eight years younger than her, funny and restless. On a December morning in 1958 he caught the 6:40 train to the city. He left a note on the kitchen table under a split pomegranate: *"When you wake up, I will already be gone. Don't wait up. Finish it for me? T."* He meant the painting. He never came back.
- **What she did afterwards.** She waited:
  - She kept a candle burning in the window every night for years, in case he came home late.
  - She kept his canary's cage after the canary, Pip, flew out of the door he left open.
  - She put the unfinished painting in the back bedroom under a sheet and never looked at it again.
  - Years later a second letter came, in a stranger's handwriting. She put it unopened in the second drawer.
- **Why the dream is the way it is.** Odile has spent sixty years not looking at something, and her dream is built around that one thing she refuses to look at.

## The layers of the dream

| Layer | What it is | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **I · The Soft Desert** | Dalí's desert of soft clocks, dead trees and a bed out in the open | Time she let slip by. She mended everyone's clocks but let her own run down. |
| **II · Golconda Piazza** | De Chirico's long shadows, arcades, a train on the horizon, and identical men in bowler hats raining from the sky | The city he left for. For years she looked for his hat in every crowd, and every crowd was the same man. |
| **III · The Unwatched** | A giant eye on impossible legs in a mirrored plaza | The painting under the sheet, and what she can't look at. You can only hurt it while you are not looking at it, because grief can only be approached sideways. |

**The Sleepwalkers** are her anxieties: faceless mannequins wrapped in sheets, each with a drawer in its chest full of things never said. The **Golconda men** are every stranger who wasn't Théo.

## The Night-Light (the narrator)

The Night-Light is the candle Odile kept in the window, and it floats beside you in a little glass jar. It has been waiting for sixty years and has opinions about it. It is dry, precise and fond of you, in the tradition of a certain testing-facility computer, but its cruelty is only ever turned on itself.

It knows the rules of the dream: take, give, don't let her notice. It won't tell you what's at the bottom. It sometimes speaks as if it were Odile, and then corrects itself. When she wakes, its last line in the run is always a little sad.

Some sample lines:
- *"Oh. You're awake. That's new. Usually it's only me."*
- *"Take the melting from the clock. Clocks don't need it. Believe me, I've seen what she does to clocks."*
- *"Careful. The more absurd you are, the more she notices. And when she notices, she wakes up. Nobody likes being noticed."*
- *"You died. Technically she woke up. From here those look identical."*
- (to the boss) *"Don't look at it. I mean it. I've been not-looking at it for sixty years and I'm very good at it."*

## The nine canvas scraps (lore to find)

Torn pieces of the painting are hidden in each layer, usually behind a parkour challenge or an absurd combo. Each one shows a line, and together they reassemble *L'Homme au Chapeau* in the journal.

1. *(Desert)* "Theo, 9: 'Odile is a man with a hat and an apple instead of a face. She says it's art. I say it's cheating.'" — a child's note, pinned to a fridge.
2. *(Desert)* "Clock repaired: Mme. Berthier's carriage clock. Loses four minutes a day. Customer says she doesn't mind. She likes the extra time." — the shop ledger, 1961.
3. *(Desert)* "The candle burned all night again. The neighbours asked if someone was ill. I said someone was late." — Odile's diary.
4. *(Piazza)* "Dear O. The city is enormous and everyone has the same hat. I painted a thousand of them falling like rain. Nobody bought it. Send the good brushes? T." — a letter, 1959.
5. *(Piazza)* "Platform 3, 6:40. I went every morning for a month. I don't know what I would have said." — Odile's diary.
6. *(Piazza)* "He painted the arches from memory. Long shadows, a train, no people. He said empty squares were the only honest places." — margin note, a gallery catalogue.
7. *(Unwatched)* "The sheet has not been lifted since 1958. I dust around it." — Odile's diary.
8. *(Unwatched)* "Second drawer. Unopened. The stamp is from the south. The handwriting isn't his." — a note to herself.
9. *(Unwatched)* "Finish it for me? T." — the corner of the pomegranate note.

## The ending

When the Unwatched finally falls, its eye closes, and in the waking vignette Odile gets up in the dark:

- She goes to the back bedroom and lifts the sheet.
- The painting is of a figure in a bowler hat with an apple where the face should be, holding nothing, standing in a desert.
- She opens the second drawer and reads the letter, which says what she already knew.
- Then she takes out the good brushes, the ones he asked for and never got, and finishes the face.
- The last line: *"It was never a man. It was her, at nine, in their father's hat, making her little brother laugh. She paints the face she had then. It takes until morning. For the first time in sixty years, she lets the candle go out."*

The Night-Light's final line: *"Well. Somebody had to finish it."*
